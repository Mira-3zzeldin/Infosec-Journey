## Answer Key & Explanations

**1. C — Tractor feed.** Tractor feed is the paper-feed mechanism unique to impact printers, using perforated continuous-feed paper pulled through by sprocket-driven edges rather than friction rollers alone.

**2. B — Solenoid.** The solenoid is the electromechanical hammer that strikes the back of a daisy-wheel's petal, pushing the raised character into the inked ribbon and onto the page.

**3. B — Characters per second (cps).** Impact printer speed, unlike laser/inkjet page-based speed ratings, is measured character by character since output is built one struck character at a time.

**4. C — Letter quality (LQ).** LQ describes daisy-wheel output quality — clearly legible, comparable to a typewriter, though not to modern inkjet standards.

**5. A — 9.** The earliest dot-matrix printheads used only nine pins, producing coarse "draft quality" output before 17-pin and 24-pin heads improved density and quality.

**6. B — ~72 cps.** Dot-matrix printers typically started around 72 characters per second, considerably faster than early daisy-wheel printers' 2–4 cps.

**7. B — 100 to 200.** A typical inkjet printhead contains roughly 100 to 200 tiny nozzles that spray ink droplets to form the printed image.

**8. B — Cyan, Magenta, Yellow, Black.** CMYK is the standard four-ink color model used by color inkjet (and laser) printers to produce a full color range.

**9. A — 512 KB to 16 MB.** This is the typical size range for a printer's onboard print buffer, which stores queued print jobs as they arrive from the host computer.

**10. B — Page printers.** Laser and inkjet printers both receive and process an entire page's worth of instructions at once, distinguishing them from older line-by-line impact printing.

**11. B — −600VDC.** The charging corona applies a strong, uniform −600VDC charge to the photosensitive drum as the first electrical step of the EP imaging process.

**12. C — +600VDC.** The transfer corona applies a +600VDC charge to the paper, which — being opposite the toner's negative charge — pulls the developed image off the drum.

**13. B — 329°F–392°F (165°C–200°C).** This is the fusing roller's normal operating temperature range, hot enough to melt toner's polyester resin into the paper fibers.

**14. C — Printer controller circuitry.** This large circuit board converts (rasterizes) computer signals into the specific commands each printer assembly needs to execute its part of the EP process.

**15. C — +24VDC.** The DCPS supplies +24VDC specifically to power the paper transport motors, separate from the +5VDC/−5VDC it provides to logic circuitry.

**16. C — Cleaning blade.** A rubber cleaning blade inside the EP cartridge continuously scrapes leftover toner off the photosensitive drum, keeping it ready for the next imaging cycle.

**17. B — A heated printhead that darkens heat-sensitive paper directly.** This describes thermal direct printing specifically; thermal transfer printing (the distractor in option A) instead melts a wax ribbon onto ordinary paper.

**18. C — 9100.** RAW/Standard TCP/IP printing uses TCP port 9100 by default, alongside SNMP for bidirectional status communication between computer and printer.

**19. B — Source 721–731, destination 515.** These are the legacy LPR protocol's defined port ranges, included mainly for compatibility with older systems.

**20. B — SMB (Server Message Block).** Scan-to-folder relies on SMB to transport the scanned file from the MFD to a shared network folder destination.

**21. A — Physical pressure from the printhead strikes through to lower carbonless layers.** Only impact printing physically presses hard enough to transfer an impression through multiple carbonless layers simultaneously — inkjet and laser methods lay material only on the top sheet.

**22. B — Dot-matrix printers combined faster speeds, quieter operation, and multipart-form compatibility.** Retaining impact printing's multipart-form advantage while adding meaningfully better speed and a dust-cover-quieted mechanism gave dot-matrix a decisive edge over daisy-wheel technology.

**23. A — The printhead is frequently packaged together with the ink reservoir as a single removable unit.** Many inkjet designs combine printhead and ink supply into one replaceable cartridge, which is part of why replacing a cartridge can feel like getting a "new printer" each time.

**24. A — Thermal-bubble vaporizes ink to push it out; piezoelectric physically flexes an element to push/pull ink.** These are the two genuinely distinct physical ejection mechanisms used across inkjet manufacturers, not interchangeable marketing terms.

**25. A — Opposite/lesser charges attract, while equally charged (like) surfaces repel each other.** Toner charged to match the developing roller (−600VDC) is drawn toward the drum's exposed, lower-charge (−100VDC) areas and repelled from areas still at the roller's own charge level.

**26. A — The charged paper would stick to the EP cartridge, causing a paper jam.** The static-charge eliminator strip exists specifically to bleed off the transfer corona's charge from the paper; without it, the charged paper would be attracted to and jam against the cartridge.

**27. B — Direct paper contact allows corona rollers to support higher print speeds, and they also produce far less ozone.** This dual advantage — speed and reduced ozone byproduct — is why corona rollers have largely displaced corona wires in modern laser printer designs.

**28. B — Leftover toner and residual drum charge would otherwise create "ghost" images bleeding into the next page.** The cleaning step exists precisely to prevent artifacts from a previous print job from carrying over into the next one.

**29. A — It shifts page-rendering processing work from the computer to the printer and reduces data sent for complex graphics.** A page-description language lets the driver send compact draw/position commands instead of exhaustive per-dot instructions, which matters most for graphics- and font-heavy jobs.

**30. A — A printer share is the shared printer itself; a print server is whatever device hosts it and processes its commands.** These are related but distinct concepts, and the exam objectives explicitly separate them under public/shared devices.

**31. A — Print jobs go directly to the printer, so the client doesn't spend processing time acting as a print server or formatting the job.** This is the core efficiency advantage of TCP/RAW printing over routing jobs through a PC-based print server.

**32. A — Bonjour's automatic discovery is limited to operating within a single broadcast domain.** Bonjour's zero-configuration discovery mechanism doesn't cross router/broadcast-domain boundaries, so devices on the far side of a router remain invisible to it.

**33. A — Mobile devices can print to compatible printers on the local network without installing a printer driver.** AirPrint's defining feature is eliminating the client-side driver requirement — the manufacturer builds AirPrint compatibility into the printer instead.

**34. A — It caches print jobs on the print server's storage indefinitely, creating a data-privacy risk.** "Keep printed documents" specifically causes hard drive caching of print jobs, which is exactly the risk this chapter warns against on public/shared devices.

**35. A — It allows multiple pages (commonly up to ~50) to be scanned in a single continuous job rather than one at a time.** An ADF's entire purpose is batch-scanning a stack of pages automatically, unlike a flatbed scanner's one-document-at-a-time workflow.

**36. A — The refill ink often has different viscosity than OEM ink, the aging printhead isn't replaced, and the puncture point can leak.** These are the three specific, concrete problems refill kits introduce on integrated cartridge/printhead inkjet designs.

**37. A — Refilling skips replacing the photosensitive drum often bundled inside the same cartridge, degrading print quality.** Since many laser toner cartridges house the drum internally, refilling just the toner leaves an aging, unreplaced drum in service.

**38. A — Sheets can stick together, leading to paper jams during feeding.** High humidity is specifically called out as a paper-jam risk factor because moisture causes sheets to cling together in the tray or feed path.

**39. A — It interferes with the printer's ability to neutralize ozone and can permanently damage toner cartridges.** Ammonia-based cleaners are specifically flagged as harmful near laser printers for exactly this reason, distinct from any electrical or thermal hazard.

**40. A — Output can appear on any printer in the pool, so users in different locations wouldn't know where to collect their job.** Printer pooling routes jobs to whichever pooled printer is available, which only works smoothly if every pooled unit sits in the same physical spot.

**41. D — Cleaning.** Ghost images bleeding through from the previous page are the textbook symptom of an EP cleaning-step failure, since leftover toner or residual drum charge wasn't fully removed before the next cycle.

**42. B — The wrong printer driver/page-description language was selected.** Garbled or literal command text printing out instead of the intended document is the classic symptom of a page-description-language mismatch between driver and printer.

**43. B — 329°F–392°F (165°C–200°C).** This is the normal fusing roller operating range; a roller failing to reach it would produce exactly the light, poorly-bonded output described.

**44. B — Secured prints (hold the job until the user authenticates at the correct physical printer).** Secured prints exists specifically to solve the "job went to the wrong physical printer" problem by holding output until the user releases it at the device they're actually standing at.

**45. C — Badging.** Badging is specifically defined as authenticating via a physical ID badge scan rather than typed credentials, exactly matching the scenario described.

**46. A — Audit logs.** Audit logs exist precisely to answer "who printed what and when" after the fact, whether stored natively on the printer or integrated into OS-level logging.

**47. A — Adjust the gap between the printhead and the ribbon.** This chapter specifically calls out printhead-to-ribbon distance adjustment as the first fix for output that's too light (move closer) or smeared (move back) on impact printers.

**48. A — Run the printer's built-in cleaning cycle to clear dried ink from the nozzles.** Streaky or incomplete lines are the standard symptom of clogged or dried nozzles, and the built-in cleaning cycle is the designed first response before considering hardware replacement.

**49. A — The print server is missing drivers for the Windows and Linux client operating systems.** A print server needs drivers on hand for every client OS that will connect; missing Windows/Linux drivers explains exactly why only macOS clients can add the printer successfully.

**50. B — Ad hoc connection.** A temporary, single-device wireless link with no intention of permanence is the definition of an ad hoc connection, distinct from a permanent infrastructure-mode network attachment.

**51. A — The user's device is too far from the wireless access point for a reliable connection.** Distance from the access point is the most direct explanation for an inconsistent or failing Wi-Fi print connection when a nearer coworker succeeds without issue.

**52. A — RAW, because it's the default, uses port 9100, and supports bidirectional status via SNMP.** RAW is the modern default protocol for TCP/IP printing, offering richer bidirectional status feedback than the legacy LPR alternative.
