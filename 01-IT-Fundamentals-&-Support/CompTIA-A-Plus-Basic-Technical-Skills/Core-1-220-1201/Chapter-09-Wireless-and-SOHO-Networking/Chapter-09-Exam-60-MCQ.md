## Answer Key & Explanations

**1. B — 1997.** The original 802.11 standard was ratified in 1997 as the first standardized WLAN implementation, transmitting at 1–2 Mbps over 2.4 GHz. No commercial devices were sold under this bare designation.

**2. C — 6 GHz.** Wi-Fi 6E, released in 2020, is functionally identical to standard Wi-Fi 6 (802.11ax) but adds support for the 6 GHz band, unlike Wi-Fi 6 which only covers 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz.

**3. B — 14.** The 2.4 GHz band defines 14 total 20 MHz channels via FCC regulation, though only the first 11 are legally usable in the United States.

**4. C — 1, 6, 11.** These are the only three 2.4 GHz channels spaced at least five channel numbers apart, making them fully nonoverlapping and the standard configuration for multi-AP deployments.

**5. B — Service-Set Identifier.** SSID is the technical name for a wireless network, used by clients to identify and connect to a specific network.

**6. B — 11 Mbps.** 802.11b delivered up to 11 Mbps (with fallback rates of 5.5, 2, and 1 Mbps) over the 2.4 GHz band using DSSS modulation.

**7. C — 2003.** 802.11g was ratified in 2003, delivering 54 Mbps over 2.4 GHz while remaining backward compatible with 802.11b.

**8. B — 9.6 Gbps.** Wi-Fi 6 (802.11ax) has a theoretical maximum PHY rate of 9.6 Gbps, up from Wi-Fi 5's 6.9 Gbps. Option A (6.9 Gbps) is Wi-Fi 5's rate, a common point of confusion.

**9. C — 4096-QAM.** Wi-Fi 7 uses 4096-QAM (4K-QAM), packing 12 bits per symbol versus 1024-QAM's 10 bits, contributing to its dramatically higher PHY rate.

**10. B — Wireless Internet Service Provider.** A WISP offers fixed, point-to-point broadband requiring line-of-sight, typically over unlicensed or licensed radio spectrum.

**11. B — It provides the route to networks outside the local subnet.** The default gateway is the address of the router that connects the local network to the outside world; without it, a device cannot reach anything beyond its own subnet.

**12. D — 169.254.0.0/16.** APIPA assigns addresses in the 169.254.x.x range when no DHCP server can be located, enabling only local subnet communication.

**13. B — 13.56 MHz.** High-frequency (HF) RFID operates at 13.56 MHz, the same frequency NFC uses, since NFC is technically a subset of HF RFID.

**14. B — 10 centimeters.** NFC's effective operating range is approximately 10 centimeters (4 inches), intentionally short to support secure proximity use cases like contactless payment.

**15. C — 10 meters.** Class 2 Bluetooth devices, the most common class for computer peripherals and headsets, have a range of approximately 10 meters and draw 2.5 mW of power.

**16. C — 2018.** WPA3 was released in 2018 and became mandatory for newly Wi-Fi-certified devices starting in July 2020.

**17. B — 100 meters.** UTP cabling has a standard maximum segment distance of 100 meters (328 feet); runs beyond that require a repeater or a switch to a fiber segment.

**18. A — DOCSIS.** Cable Internet delivers broadband access via the Data Over Cable Service Interface Specification (DOCSIS), distinguishing it from DSL's phone-line-based approach.

**19. C — UDP 1900.** UPnP's Simple Service Discovery Protocol operates over UDP port 1900, and the underlying client must also be a DHCP client.

**20. B — Access Control List.** An ACL is the rule set a firewall uses to determine which traffic is permitted or blocked, typically filtering by IP address, port, or domain.

**21. B — They operate on different frequency bands.** 802.11a transmits at 5 GHz while 802.11b transmits at 2.4 GHz; devices must share both frequency and modulation to interoperate, so these two standards cannot communicate directly.

**22. B — Both operate in the 2.4 GHz band and share compatible modulation options.** 802.11g shares 802.11b's frequency and can use compatible modulation (DSSS), which is precisely what allowed it to remain backward compatible and replace 802.11b in the field.

**23. B — Channel bonding and MIMO.** 802.11n combined wider 40 MHz channels (via channel bonding) with multiple-input multiple-output antenna technology to significantly boost throughput over prior standards.

**24. C — It helps offset the shorter inherent range of the 5 GHz band by directing signal at the client.** Beamforming targets the signal at a specific client rather than broadcasting omnidirectionally, compensating for 5 GHz's naturally shorter range compared to 2.4 GHz.

**25. A — It allows communication with several clients simultaneously instead of one at a time.** OFDMA, introduced with Wi-Fi 6, overcomes OFDM's limitation of only transmitting to one recipient at a time, letting an access point serve multiple clients concurrently.

**26. A — Wi-Fi 5's MU-MIMO only supported downlink, so the router could send to multiple clients but not receive from multiple clients at once.** Wi-Fi 6 extended MU-MIMO to uplink as well, letting the router simultaneously receive from multiple clients, reducing latency and improving multi-device performance.

**27. B — Co-channel interference between access points sharing a channel.** BSS coloring tags wireless frames so an access point can distinguish its own traffic from that of a same-channel neighbor, directly addressing co-channel interference.

**28. A — DFS-restricted channels must be reserved to avoid conflicting with radar systems.** Weather, commercial, and military radar share the 5 GHz spectrum, so DFS dynamically detects and avoids those channels, which reduces the pool of freely usable nonoverlapping channels.

**29. B — A wireless router can act as a DHCP server and manage network security, while a WAP cannot.** A WAP is functionally the wireless analog of a wired switch — it connects clients but doesn't provide the DHCP or security management features a wireless router offers.

**30. C — Technology improvements and signal-hopping have made interference unlikely, though not impossible.** Both technologies share unlicensed 2.4 GHz spectrum, but modern signal-hopping and modulation improvements have made real-world interference between them uncommon rather than eliminated.

**31. B — Active tags have their own power source rather than relying on the reader's radio waves.** Because active RFID tags carry their own battery (and sometimes their own antenna), they can transmit farther (~100 meters) than passive tags, which draw power entirely from the reader's radio waves (~25 meters).

**32. B — A wireless packet sniffer can still discover the SSID; hiding it only inconveniences legitimate clients.** Disabling SSID broadcast does not encrypt or protect the network — the SSID remains discoverable to anyone using basic wireless monitoring tools, so it provides no real security benefit.

**33. B — Enterprise mode centralizes authentication through a dedicated authentication server rather than the device handling it locally.** WPA2/WPA3 Enterprise offload authentication to a RADIUS server, which is what allows centralized credential management across many users, unlike Personal mode where the access point itself handles authentication.

**34. C — Default-deny is more secure but requires more configuration effort to permit legitimate traffic.** A default-deny firewall blocks everything unless explicitly authorized, offering strong security at the cost of upfront configuration work; default-allow is the opposite tradeoff.

**35. B — To make the server, which lacks its own public IP, reachable from the Internet through address translation.** DNAT translates a pool of private addresses to routable addresses, which is specifically useful for exposing an internal resource like a web server that doesn't have its own public IP address.

**36. A — Fiber transmits data as light rather than electrical signal.** Because fiber-optic cable carries data as pulses of light rather than electrical current, it is inherently immune to electromagnetic interference, unlike copper cabling.

**37. C — Cat 6 supports 10 Gbps at similar cost to Cat 5e, offering better future upgradeability.** Since Cat 6 costs roughly the same as Cat 5e while supporting significantly higher throughput (10 Gbps versus 1 Gbps), it's the more future-proof choice for new installations.

**38. B — The signal must travel roughly 35,000 km to the satellite and back.** Satellite's propagation delay (250–350 ms) stems directly from the physical distance the signal must travel to reach orbit and return, far exceeding the 10–30 ms delay typical of cable or DSL.

**39. B — mmWave signals are limited to about half a mile and are easily obstructed, requiring closely spaced transmitters.** mmWave's short range and vulnerability to obstruction (walls, trees, even a hand) mean transmitters must be densely packed, which is only cost-effective in already-dense urban environments.

**40. A — Static IP clients cannot use DHCP-assigned DNS servers, and clients cannot broadcast to locate a DNS server the way they can for DHCP.** Since a static client bypasses DHCP entirely, and DNS servers cannot be discovered via broadcast the way DHCP servers can, the DNS address must be entered manually alongside the static IP configuration.

**41. C — Channels 1, 6, and 11.** With three WAPs experiencing interference in the 2.4 GHz band, only channels 1, 6, and 11 provide the required nonoverlapping spacing to eliminate co-channel conflicts.

**42. B — Data rate naturally decreases with distance and weaker signal strength, even though the PHY rate stays the same.** The PHY rate is a fixed theoretical ceiling for the standard in use, but the actual achievable data rate degrades with distance, signal strength, and interference — exactly what the distant user is experiencing.

**43. B — Use a device that supports Power over Ethernet (PoE).** PoE delivers both power and network connectivity over a single Ethernet cable, making it the standard solution for connectivity devices placed where no electrical outlet is available.

**44. B — The DNS server address is missing or incorrect.** Successful pings by IP address confirm basic connectivity and routing are working, but the inability to load sites by name specifically points to a DNS resolution failure rather than a gateway or subnet issue.

**45. B — WPA3-Enterprise with a RADIUS server.** WPA3-Enterprise offers the strongest currently available wireless security, and pairing it with a RADIUS server enables the centralized authentication management appropriate for a large organization.

**46. B — Clone the old router's MAC address onto the new router.** Most wireless routers support MAC address cloning, letting the new device present the old router's MAC address to the ISP so the existing connection continues to work without contacting the provider.

**47. B — Assign the APs to channels 1, 6, and 11 instead.** Overlapping coverage combined with identical channels causes co-channel interference; reassigning the APs to the three nonoverlapping 2.4 GHz channels resolves the disconnects.

**48. B — The laptop could not reach a DHCP server and self-assigned an APIPA address.** A 169.254.x.x address is the signature of Windows' automatic APIPA fallback, which occurs specifically when no DHCP server responds, and it only supports local subnet communication.

**49. B — Place the web server in a screened subnet and keep the database server on the internal network.** Internet-facing resources like a web server belong in a screened subnet reachable from outside, while resources that don't need external access, like a database server, should remain fully on the protected internal network.

**50. B — The ISP's bandwidth throttling policy after an initial speed boost.** Many cable providers boost download speed for the first 10–20 MB of a transfer and then throttle back to the plan's normal sustained rate, exactly matching the described symptom.

**51. C — Satellite.** With no line-of-sight to a WISP tower and no cabled broadband available, satellite — which only requires a clear view of the sky toward the orbiting relay — is the most realistic remaining option.

**52. B — High propagation delay (latency) inherent to satellite's long signal path.** Fast download bandwidth doesn't offset satellite's inherent latency, which is what specifically degrades real-time interactive applications like online gaming, independent of download speed.

**53. B — Plenum-rated cable.** Any cable run through an air-handling space, such as a drop ceiling doubling as an HVAC return, must be plenum-rated, since standard PVC-jacketed cable releases toxic gas when burned.

**54. B — Disable UPnP, particularly on the router's external-facing interface, since it lacks any authentication mechanism.** UPnP has no built-in authentication, meaning any device can request port openings without approval; disabling it (especially on the WAN side) is the standard mitigation.

**55. A — The default gateway.** Since the user can't reach anything outside the local subnet by IP address at all (not just by name), the missing piece is the default gateway, which provides the route out of the local network — a DNS-only problem would still allow direct IP access to work.

**56. B — Port forwarding on ports 80 and 443 to the server's internal address.** Port forwarding (the common many-to-one NAT scenario also known as PAT) is exactly what's needed to route specific inbound ports from the single public IP to a specific internal server.

**57. B — Port triggering.** Port triggering is the ACL mechanism that automatically opens a specific inbound port after a matching outbound request on that same port, exactly matching the RDP example described.

**58. B — Same SSID on each AP, 10–20% overlapping coverage, and nonoverlapping channels.** Proper multi-AP roaming requires identical SSIDs across access points, coverage overlap of at least 10 percent (20 percent recommended) to avoid dead spots, and nonoverlapping channels to prevent interference.

**59. B — WPA3-Personal (using SAE).** WPA3-Personal offers the strongest currently available security without requiring a RADIUS server or enterprise infrastructure, using SAE to resist dictionary attacks even with weaker passwords.
