# ANSWER KEY WITH EXPLANATIONS

---
---

**1. C — VGA.** VGA connectors are nearly universally colored blue, and VGA is explicitly the only purely analog connector among the Exam Objective 3.2 video standards (VGA, DVI, HDMI, DisplayPort, USB-C).

**2. B — 480 Mbps.** USB 2.0, released in 2000, carries the trade name "Hi-Speed" and tops out at 480 Mbps — a huge jump from USB 1.1's 12 Mbps but far below USB 3.0's 5 Gbps.

**3. B — 7.** SATA data cables use a 7-pin connector; the separate SATA power cable uses 15 pins and is physically wider, a common point of confusion for new technicians.

**4. C — SuperSpeed.** USB 3.0 (2008, 5 Gbps) carries the trade name SuperSpeed; USB 3.1 and 3.2 both use "SuperSpeed+" despite different actual speeds (10 Gbps and 20 Gbps respectively).

**5. C — 2012.** The Lightning connector debuted with the iPhone 5 in 2012, replacing Apple's older 30-pin dock connector with a slimmer 8-pin design.

**6. B.** The inverter is a small circuit board that converts DC current into the AC required by a fluorescent LCD backlight; it does not produce light itself, and dim/flickering symptoms typically point to inverter failure rather than the backlight bulb.

**7. B — 4.** The classic Molex connector used to power PATA drives and older optical drives is a 4-pin connector, historically white/opaque plastic but with no official mandated color.

**8. B — Capacitive.** Capacitive touchscreens sense electrical conductivity (a bare finger works well), and typically fail with gloves since fabric/rubber don't conduct electricity the way skin does; resistive screens use pressure instead and work fine with gloves or a stylus.

**9. C — 2008.** DisplayPort, developed by VESA, was introduced in 2008 as a royalty-free replacement effort targeting both VGA and DVI.

**10. B — 3.** A TRS connector has three conductors corresponding to Tip (left audio), Ring (right audio), and Sleeve (ground); adding a fourth conductor for microphone input makes it a TRRS connector instead.

**11. B — Lightning.** The Lightning connector is Apple's proprietary 8-pin connector, notably unkeyed (reversible), distinguishing it from USB-C (also reversible, but an open industry standard) and Micro-USB (keyed, one orientation only).

**12. B — Keyboard, Video, Mouse.** A KVM switch allows a single set of keyboard, video (monitor), and mouse peripherals to be shared/switched among multiple connected computers.

**13. B — 3 meters.** USB 3.x cables are limited to 3 meters maximum, shorter than USB 1.x/2.0's 5-meter limit and longer than USB4's 80cm limit — reflecting the tighter signal integrity tolerances of higher-speed standards.

**14. D — OLED.** OLED pixels are individually self-illuminating via an organic compound between two electrodes, eliminating the need for any backlight — unlike TN, IPS, and VA, which are all LCD variants requiring a separate backlight source.

**15. B — Serial port.** DB-9 is the classic connector shape for legacy serial (RS-232) ports; it is technically distinct from the RS-232 communication standard itself, though the terms are casually used interchangeably.

**16. B — Purple.** Legacy PS/2 keyboard connectors and ports were color-coded purple, while PS/2 mouse connectors/ports were color-coded green.

**17. C — 240W.** USB Power Delivery 3.1 (released 2021) supports up to 48V at 5A, totaling 240W — and requires a USB-C cable to achieve this maximum.

**18. B — 2002.** HDMI was introduced in 2002, advancing DVI's video capability while adding digital audio and (later) Ethernet and CEC remote-control features.

**19. C — USB-C.** Thunderbolt switched from the Mini DisplayPort connector (used in Thunderbolt 1 and 2) to the now-standard USB-C connector shape starting with Thunderbolt 3 in 2015.

**20. B — 2003.** eSATA entered the market in 2003, the same year as the first SATA revision (1.5 Gbps), and is primarily intended for external hard drive connectivity.

**21. B.** Running a non-native resolution forces the display to interpolate the differing pixel count onto its fixed physical transistor/cell grid, typically resulting in a distorted or blurry image rather than a crisp one.

**22. B.** USB is limited by the weakest link in the chain — a USB 1.1-rated cable cannot carry USB 2.0 speeds, so the connected device falls back to whatever maximum the cable itself supports, sacrificing performance but usually still functioning.

**23. B.** DVI-A and VGA are explicitly pin-compatible, meaning a simple, inexpensive passive adapter (no active conversion chip needed) is sufficient to bridge the two — because both are carrying the same analog signal type, just through different physical connector shapes.

**24. C.** DVI does not convert between analog and digital signal types under any cable configuration — DVI-A (analog) and DVI-D (digital) are fundamentally incompatible signal types, and no passive or even DVI-I cable can bridge that gap; the devices themselves would need matching signal type support.

**25. B.** `LnkSta` reporting half the `LnkCap` speed (8GT/s vs. 16GT/s capability) at full lane width indicates the link negotiated down to a lower PCIe generation than the hardware is capable of, which effectively halves available bandwidth — a genuine real-world performance bottleneck worth investigating (driver, BIOS setting, or slot issue).

**26. B.** USB's addressing scheme uses a 7-bit identifier, yielding 2⁷=128 possible unique addresses; the 128th (highest) address is reserved for broadcasting to all endpoints, leaving 127 usable device addresses per host controller.

**27. B.** The monitor always has final authority over the signal it receives — if a monitor supports only one refresh rate, that's the only option available regardless of how many rates the graphics adapter can technically output.

**28. B.** Higher resolution requires transmitting significantly more pixel data per frame; on some monitor/video-card/cable combinations, this additional bandwidth demand causes the highest available refresh rates to disappear from the selection menu at increased resolutions, forcing a resolution/refresh-rate tradeoff.

**29. B.** A NAS's USB (or eSATA) connection to a host PC exists primarily to run the NAS's own configuration software — actual file access for network users happens over the NAS's network connection instead, not through that USB/eSATA link.

**30. C.** Thunderbolt both provides power to attached devices (via copper active cables, up to 100W or more depending on version) and explicitly supports daisy-chaining up to six levels deep per controller interface — a combination eSATA, VGA, and DB-9 serial do not share.

**31. B.** A yellow-colored USB port specifically denotes an "always-on" charging port, capable of delivering power to a connected device even while the host system is asleep or completely powered off.

**32. A.** The third SATA generation (6.0 Gbps) is branded "SATA 6" instead of "SATA 3" specifically to avoid ambiguity with the second generation's 3.0 Gbps speed rating — two competing numbering conventions (revision count vs. speed) would otherwise collide confusingly.

**33. B.** Thunderbolt 4 (2020) kept the same 40 Gbps maximum bandwidth ceiling as Thunderbolt 3 (2015) — its actual improvements were a mandatory minimum PCIe throughput increase (32 Gbps, up from 16 Gbps) and multi-display support (two 4k displays or one 8k display), not raw speed.

**34. B.** HDMI is a digital-only source signal; driving analog VGA/DVI-A hardware from it requires an active adapter containing real conversion electronics (powered externally or via the HDMI port itself) — a passive adapter cannot perform digital-to-analog conversion.

**35. B.** Capacitive touchscreens rely on the electrical conductivity of a bare finger and typically fail to register touches through gloves (which insulate that conductivity), whereas resistive touchscreens respond to simple physical pressure and work regardless of glove use or stylus.

**36. A.** DisplayPort connectors specifically include beveled keying plus a physical dual-hook latching mechanism (released via a push-button) designed to resist accidental disconnection under cable movement or desk vibration — the technician should verify the latch fully engaged, not assume a fault.

**37. B.** PATA drives (and older optical drives) are powered via the 4-pin Molex connector, not SATA power, the 24-pin ATX main connector (which powers the motherboard), or eSATAp (an external hybrid port for eSATA devices).

**38. C.** VGA is explicitly analog-only among the Exam Objective 3.2 video connectors; DVI-D is explicitly digital-only (the opposite pairing given in option B is incorrect), and HDMI is an all-digital standard, making option D also incorrect.

**39. D.** Per the chapter's brightness guidance, a fully lit business environment with no ambient light control requires roughly 5,000–6,000 lumens, compared to as little as 1,300 lumens in a fully light-controlled home theater setting.

**40. A.** Optical Thunderbolt cables transmit signal via light through fiber-optic strands rather than electrical current through copper conductors, meaning there is no electrical pathway available to deliver power to the attached device — only copper Thunderbolt cables can carry both signal and power.

**41. B.** Flickering and dimness symptoms on a fluorescent-backlit LCD are the classic signature of a failing inverter (which converts DC to the AC needed by the fluorescent backlight tube) rather than the crystal layer, cable, or GPU memory — inverter failure is the standard first suspect for this exact symptom pair.

**42. B.** USB connections are bottlenecked by the weakest component in the chain; plugging a USB 3.0-rated device into a USB 2.0 port forces the connection to fall back to USB 2.0's maximum 480 Mbps ceiling, explaining the reduced transfer speed without indicating any actual hardware fault.

**43. B.** Achieving a monitor's full advertised refresh rate (240 Hz) requires an interface/cable combination with sufficient bandwidth; an older or lower-bandwidth cable/connector can cap the achievable refresh rate well below the monitor's true maximum capability, matching this scenario's symptoms.

**44. B.** Modern USB-C ports frequently support DisplayPort Alt Mode for native video output; combined with an active USB-C-to-VGA adapter (necessary since VGA is analog and USB-C's native signal is digital), this is the correct practical solution, assuming the laptop's specific USB-C port supports video output.

**45. B.** Capacitive touchscreens depend on detecting the electrical conductivity of a bare finger; gloves (especially insulating winter gloves) block that conductivity path, causing touches to go unregistered — this is the textbook capacitive-vs-glove failure scenario.

**46. B.** USB-IF recommends (but does not mandate) blue for USB 3.0+ ports; manufacturers are free to deviate, so mixed port colors on a single board are common and don't necessarily indicate malfunction — the port's printed label (e.g., "USB 3.2") is the authoritative source of truth, not color alone.

**47. B.** A `LnkSta` reporting full rated speed (16GT/s, matching `LnkCap`) but reduced lane width (x4 instead of a capable x16) indicates the physical link is running at the correct generation but with far fewer lanes than it's capable of — a classic real-world bottleneck caused by lane-sharing slot configurations or poor physical seating, not a driver or BIOS firmware issue.

**48. C.** A 60W laptop charging requirement falls comfortably within USB Power Delivery 1.0's 100W ceiling (or any subsequent PD revision at or above that threshold); the earlier USB Battery Charging standards (7.5W and 20W max) fall far short of what's needed.

**49. B.** The 15-pin D-sub connector used exclusively for analog display output is the DE-15F, more commonly called HD-15 or "VGA" in casual industry terminology — distinct from DB-9 (serial), DB-25 (parallel), and PS/2 (keyboard/mouse, round mini-DIN).

**50. B.** Since the NAS's status lights and direct USB configuration link both function normally, the fault is isolated away from the NAS hardware itself and points toward the network-side connection (cable, port, or switch) that all other network users rely on for file access.

**51. B.** Three visible black bands on an audio connector indicate four total conductors (Tip, Ring, Ring, Sleeve), making it a TRRS connector — the extra fourth conductor beyond a standard TRS jack is specifically what enables simultaneous microphone input alongside stereo audio output.

**52.
