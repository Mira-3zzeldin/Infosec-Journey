# ANSWER KEY WITH EXPLANATIONS

---

**1. A** — NIC stands for Network Interface Card, the expansion card (or integrated circuitry) that connects a computer to a network.

**2. B** — Classic sound card jacks are small, round 1/8" (3.55mm) connectors used for microphones, headphones, and speakers.

**3. C** — Pink is the standard color code for microphone input on a sound card's back panel.

**4. C** — SATA 3.x (SATA-600), released in 2009, has a maximum transfer rate of 600 MBps, double that of SATA 2.x.

**5. C** — NVMe was released in 2011 by a consortium including Intel, Samsung, Dell, SanDisk, and Seagate as an open standard for fast data transfer.

**6. B** — mSATA uses the same physical layout as Mini PCI Express: a 30mm-wide, 52-pin connector, though the two use different wiring/protocols.

**7. C** — The M.2 connector uses 66 pins, most commonly on a card that is 22mm wide.

**8. B** — RAID 0 is known as disk striping, spreading data equally across at least two drives for improved speed but zero fault tolerance.

**9. B** — RAID 5 requires a minimum of three drives, since data is striped across n-1 disks with parity on the remaining disk.

**10. C** — RAID 6 requires a minimum of four drives to implement its double-parity scheme.

**11. C** — A standard SD card measures 32mm × 24mm, the same footprint as the older MMC format it's based on.

**12. B** — microSD cards measure 15mm × 11mm, the smallest of the common SD card variants.

**13. B** — A single-sided, single-layer DVD holds 4.7 GB of data.

**14. C** — A single-sided, single-layer Blu-ray disc holds 25 GB, using a violet laser for higher data density than DVD.

**15. A, B, D, E** — Standard PC power supplies output +3.3VDC, +5VDC, +12VDC, and −12VDC; +9VDC is not a standard PSU output rail.

**16. B** — Standard US wall outlets used for most electronics, including PCs, supply 110–120V AC.

**17. B** — The modern ATX12V 2.x / EPS12V motherboard connector uses 24 pins (sometimes split as a 20+4 pin design), up from the original 20-pin ATX connector.

**18. C** — The 4-pin Molex connector, keyed with two beveled corners, was the standard power connector for PATA hard drives and older optical drives.

**19. B** — The 80 PLUS certification program launched in 2004 to rate power supply energy efficiency.

**20. C** — Base 80 PLUS certification requires at least 80% efficiency at 20%, 50%, and 100% of rated load.

**21. B** — A 7,200 rpm SATA hard drive sustains approximately 100 MBps read speed, comparable to a PATA ATA/100 drive of the same spindle speed.

**22. B** — Shingled Magnetic Recording (SMR) overlaps tracks to increase capacity and reduce cost, at the expense of write performance, making it better suited to archival use than CMR.

**23. B** — Onboard video cards typically share system RAM with the CPU rather than using dedicated VRAM, which limits achievable graphics quality compared to an add-on card with its own GPU and VRAM.

**24. C** — 1 GBps per lane, times four lanes, times two for bidirectional transfer, equals 8 GBps total — matching the PCIe 3.0 x4 entry in the throughput table.

**25. B** — NVMe is an open protocol/standard, not a physical connector; it can operate over SATA, PCIe, or M.2 physical interfaces, meaning there is no dedicated "NVMe connector."

**26. C** — Even though the drive itself supports NVMe, being connected via a SATA III interface caps its real-world throughput at SATA III's 600 MBps ceiling.

**27. B** — SAS connectors have a plastic block between the data and power connector sections, while SATA connectors have a gap in the same location — the primary way to visually distinguish them.

**28. A** — SATA drives can be connected to a SAS controller and will generally work, though at SATA speeds; SAS drives cannot be connected to a SATA controller at all.

**29. C** — A B+M keyed slot accepts B-keyed, M-keyed, or B+M-keyed drives, since the socket includes both keying notches.

**30. A** — Both M.2 SATA drives and standard 2.5" SATA SSDs are limited by the SATA III interface ceiling of roughly 600 MBps, so the M.2 form factor offers a size advantage but no meaningful speed advantage in this case.

**31. C** — RAID 5 capacity equals the sum of all drives minus one drive's worth of capacity for parity; four 2 TB drives yield 3 × 2 TB = 6 TB usable.

**32. A** — RAID 0 improves performance through striping but provides zero fault tolerance, while RAID 1 provides fault tolerance through mirroring but offers no performance improvement and costs double the storage.

**33. B** — RAID 6 adds a second parity stripe compared to RAID 5's single parity stripe, allowing the array to survive two simultaneous drive failures instead of just one.

**34. B** — RAID 5 loses only the equivalent of one drive's capacity to parity (regardless of array size), which is proportionally far less wasted space than RAID 1 or RAID 10's fixed 50% loss, especially as more drives are added to the array.

**35. B** — Warm-swappable devices maintain an active filesystem and require the Safely Remove Hardware and Eject Media utility to stop them cleanly before removal, but the system itself remains powered on throughout — distinguishing them from devices with no filesystem that can simply be unplugged.

**36. B** — Longer ground leads make contact first and break contact last during insertion/removal, ensuring a power lead is never live without an established ground path, which would otherwise risk damaging the drive.

**37. C** — A single-sided, single-layer Blu-ray disc (25 GB) comfortably holds a 20 GB file, while even a double-sided DVD (9.4 GB) does not have enough capacity.

**38. B** — DC current is more likely to cause prolonged clamping of muscles (making it hard to let go of the source), while AC current is more likely to fibrillate the heart; both are potentially lethal at sufficient current levels.

**39. B** — The rise of PCIe devices, which draw significantly more power than legacy PCI devices, required additional positive voltage leads beyond what the original 20-pin ATX connector could provide, leading to the 24-pin (or 20+4) ATX12V 2.0/EPS12V standard.

**40. A** — A fully modular PSU allows every cable to be attached only as needed, while a semi-modular PSU hardwires just the motherboard and CPU cables (the ones virtually every build needs) and leaves peripheral cables detachable.

**41. B** — Modern high-end video cards are frequently dual-slot to quad-slot in width to manage larger GPUs and cooling systems, which can physically cover and block use of adjacent expansion slots on the motherboard.

**42. B** — A yellow exclamation point in Device Manager signals a driver problem; updating or reinstalling the driver is the standard first troubleshooting step before considering hardware replacement.

**43. B** — Roll Back Driver is only enabled when Windows has a previous driver version cached from an earlier update; a freshly imaged system with only one driver ever installed has nothing to roll back to.

**44. B** — Wireless NICs require the connecting access point or router to already be configured with the correct network name and security parameters before the NIC itself can establish a connection — this is a prerequisite, not a NIC malfunction.

**45. C** — NVMe M.2 SSDs use the PCIe bus rather than SATA, bypassing the roughly 600 MBps SATA III ceiling that limits both SATA SSDs and M.2 SATA SSDs, delivering dramatically higher sequential read speeds.

**46. C** — Even when a drive is properly recognized by the OS after boot, actually booting from an NVMe drive requires explicit motherboard/BIOS support for that specific slot; PCIe-slot-installed NVMe drives are more likely to lack boot support than those in a dedicated M.2 slot.

**47. D** — RAID 10 combines striping (speed) with mirroring (fault tolerance), and with eight drives available, it delivers both strong performance and solid redundancy, better balancing speed and fault tolerance than RAID 5/6 alone, even though it sacrifices half the total capacity.

**48. B** — A RAID 5 array with one failed drive is running in a degraded state — still fully functional and readable using the remaining data and parity — but it cannot survive a second drive failure before the array is rebuilt.

**49. C** — While SAS and SATA are electronically compatible enough for a SATA drive to technically function on a SAS controller, mixing SAS and SATA drives in the same array is explicitly discouraged due to the performance inconsistencies it introduces.

**50. B** — M.2 slots and cards are physically keyed by letter (A, B, E, M, etc.), and a card with a different key notch position than the slot supports simply will not fit — an A/E-keyed Wi-Fi card is not compatible with an M-keyed-only slot.

**51. B** — Windows and other operating systems often cache write operations to a device and flush them later; removing a device before this cache is flushed (by skipping Safely Remove Hardware) can result in incomplete writes and file corruption.

**52. B** — A redundant power supply protects only against the failure of the PSU hardware itself; it offers no protection against a total loss of incoming power from the wall, which requires a battery-backed UPS instead.

**53. B** — If the voltage selector switch is left on the higher 220–240V setting after moving to a 110–120V US outlet, the PSU expects more voltage than it's receiving and typically will not power up at all.

**54. B** — Leaving the switch set to the lower voltage while actually connected to a higher-voltage European outlet feeds the PSU roughly double the voltage it's configured for, which can cause sparking or fire — a genuinely dangerous scenario, unlike the safer "won't power on" failure mode of the reverse mistake.

**55. B** — In a multi-rail 12V design, each rail has its own individual capacity limit; a single rail (e.g., one dedicated to the GPU) can be overdrawn and trigger a protective shutdown even though the PSU's combined total wattage rating is more than sufficient for the whole system.

**56. A** — Modular PSU connectors add physical connection points where cables plug into the PSU itself, which can add up to roughly a half-inch of extra depth compared to a fixed-cable design — a meaningful amount of clearance in a small-form-factor case.

**57. C** — For a high-wattage system that runs many hours per day, the efficiency gains of 80 PLUS Gold or higher translate into measurable electricity savings and reduced heat/wear over the system's lifetime, making it the better long-term cost-effective choice over base or Bronze certification.

**58. B** — Skipping proper grounding before handling a sensitive component risks electrostatic discharge (ESD), which can silently damage the delicate electronic circuitry on a drive's exposed board.

**59. A** — Using only a single screw on one side allows the drive to slide within the bay and rotate around that single screw point, which can misalign connectors, stress cables, or cause the drive to shift out of position over time — at least two screws on one side are recommended.

**60. B** — When both integrated and add-on video are present, the add-on card typically takes priority for output, so a common no-display cause is the monitor cable being plugged into the now-inactive integrated video port instead of the add-on card's port, or the add-on card not being fully seated in its slot.
