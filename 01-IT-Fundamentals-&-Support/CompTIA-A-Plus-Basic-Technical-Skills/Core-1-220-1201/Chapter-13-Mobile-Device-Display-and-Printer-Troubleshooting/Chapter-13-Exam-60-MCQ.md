## Answer Key & Explanations

**1. B — LCD cutoff switch.** This is the legacy term for the display-toggle function key, dating back to when laptop panels were exclusively LCD; the term persists even on modern LED/OLED laptop displays.

**2. A — Incorrect data source.** A display configured to read from the wrong input port shows nothing, even with a perfectly functional cable and source device.

**3. C — OLED.** OLED panels use individual organic pixels that degrade under prolonged static images, making them uniquely susceptible to burn-in among current display technologies.

**4. B — Degaussing.** Degaussing decreases or eliminates unwanted magnetic fields on CRT displays, correcting the color-mapping errors that magnetism can cause.

**5. C — HDMI.** HDMI carries both audio and video over a single cable; DVI carries video only.

**6. B — Overheating.** Projectors generate significant heat, and a thermal safety cutoff shutting the unit down is the most likely cause of intermittent shutdown.

**7. C — Low-resolution mode.** This is the current Windows terminology; older versions referred to the same basic fallback video mode as VGA mode.

**8. B — Disconnect the battery from the motherboard, wait, then reconnect it.** This is the recommended first fix for a laptop that works plugged in but not on battery, before assuming outright battery replacement is needed.

**9. A — Settings > System > Power.** This is where Windows' built-in power-saving profiles are configured to extend battery life.

**10. B — Take it to a proper recycling center.** Swollen or failed batteries must never go in household trash due to explosion and environmental risk.

**11. B — Lower-left corner.** This is the standard location for the Fn key on the large majority of laptop keyboards.

**12. B — Ghost cursor.** This term specifically describes erratic pointer jumps caused by accidental palm contact with the touchpad while typing.

**13. B — Control Panel > Tablet PC Settings > Calibrate.** This is the specific Windows path for digitizer calibration on touch-enabled devices; iOS and Android offer no equivalent calibration menu.

**14. B — Whether the network card's hardware/function-key toggle is switched on.** This is the first step in this chapter's laptop wireless troubleshooting order, ahead of checking software-level enablement or signal strength.

**15. B — Solid-particle resistance, then moisture resistance.** The first IP-rating digit (0–6) covers solids like dust; the second digit (0–8) covers moisture.

**16. D — 7.** A moisture rating of 7 or higher (7 or 8) is required for a device to be classified as waterproof rather than merely water-resistant.

**17. B — Rooting.** Rooting is the Android-side equivalent of iOS jailbreaking, similarly removing built-in security restrictions.

**18. B — Identify malware symptoms.** This is the first of the five ordered steps, confirming the actual issue before quarantining, remediating, scheduling, or educating.

**19. C — -600VDC.** The charging corona applies a uniform -600VDC negative charge to the photosensitive drum during the charging stage.

**20. D — +24V.** Pin 9 on the J210 interface should read +24V; pin 1 reads +5V and pin 5 reads -5V.

**21. B — A failing display unit.** A resolution-independent warped/trapezoidal shape points to a hardware fault in the display itself rather than a settings issue, distinguishing it from the other resolution-driven distortions covered in this chapter.

**22. A — A focus issue rather than a resolution issue.** Projectors specifically have dedicated focus mechanisms (autofocus, onscreen menu, or manual ring), and a soft image on a projector is generally a focus problem distinct from LCD-panel causes like resolution or interference.

**23. B — A refresh-rate-driven flicker versus a hardware-driven flicker are different root causes with different fixes.** This chapter distinguishes a subtle flicker from too-low a refresh rate (a software fix) from backlight/inverter failure (a hardware fix) — raising the refresh rate only addresses the former.

**24. B — An aspect-ratio mismatch between resolution and the external display's native ratio.** Forcing a 16:9 resolution onto a 16:10-native display (or vice versa) produces stretching or compression, exactly as this chapter describes for aspect-ratio mismatches.

**25. B — Replace the battery.** With calibration already attempted and normal (not abnormally heavy) usage confirmed, replacement is the next appropriate step per this chapter's battery-health troubleshooting flow.

**26. B — Try a different, known-good power adapter.** With the outlet already ruled out and the adapter light confirmed lit, a failing adapter (which do fail on occasion) is the next most likely suspect before jumping to battery replacement.

**27. B — Palm contact with the touchpad during typing.** This exact symptom — erratic jumps specifically while typing — is the textbook description of a ghost cursor caused by palm contact, not a point-stick or digitizer issue.

**28. B — Disable the wireless adapter so the wired adapter can obtain an address.** This chapter explains that an active wireless connection on a shared card can block the wired path from reaching DHCP; disabling wireless resolves it.

**29. B — It has an unknown dust rating and a moisture rating suggesting some water resistance, but is not rated waterproof.** IPX6 means dust resistance was never tested (X) and moisture resistance is rated at 6 — below the level-7 minimum required to call a device waterproof.

**30. B — Jailbreaking removing built-in security restrictions, combined with installing an app outside the vetted official store.** Both factors compound the risk: jailbreaking strips built-in protections, and sideloading skips the app-store vetting process that catches most malicious apps.

**31. A — Corrupted install file, insufficient permissions, or insufficient memory.** With storage and OS compatibility already ruled out, this chapter narrows an "installs but won't open" laptop app failure to exactly these three remaining causes.

**32. B — A slipping ribbon-advance gear.** This specific symptom — fading dark-to-light consistently across each line — is the named cause-and-effect pairing for this exact fault in the impact printer quality table.

**33. B — The main motor.** The main motor advances paper and drives line spacing; the carriage motor instead affects horizontal character spacing, so uneven line spacing without character distortion points to the main motor.

**34. B — Dried, clogged ink nozzles.** Ink left unused for a week or two dries and blocks the printhead's microscopic nozzles, producing exactly this thin-blank-line symptom.

**35. B — Cartridges are not designed to be refilled and doing so risks leaks, wrong ink type, and quality loss.** This is the chapter's explicit warning against refilling, covering exactly the symptoms described (leaking, inconsistent quality).

**36. C — The high-voltage power supply (HVPS).** With the transfer corona wire already confirmed intact, and a properly-formed image present on the drum during self-test, the HVPS — which powers the transfer step — is the remaining suspect.

**37. B — A groove or scratch running around the EP drum's circumference.** A full-length, consistently-positioned black line matches this chapter's description of a drum groove that toner sticks to as though the area were discharged.

**38. B — An incorrect printer driver or page-description language setting.** This chapter specifically notes that a driver/PDL mismatch produces readable-but-nonsensical English, distinguishing it from a formatter board fault, which instead produces wavy lines or dot patterns.

**39. B — The printer landed on a different subnet after relocation.** This chapter explicitly calls out physical relocation as a cause of a printer ending up on a different subnet, breaking connectivity even though the printer itself is otherwise fine.

**40. B — Stop and restart the Print Spooler service.** Persistent failed jobs in Event Viewer combined with a job stuck indefinitely is exactly the scenario this chapter ties to a stalled spooler, resolved by restarting the service.

**41. B — Whether the projector is set to the correct input source.** With power, connection, and cabling all already confirmed working, an incorrect input source is the next item in this chapter's three-step video-input checklist.

**42. B — The set resolution doesn't match the display's native aspect ratio.** 1600×1024 does not match the 16:9 ratio of the native 2560×1440 resolution, producing exactly the stretching/compression this chapter describes for aspect-ratio mismatches.

**43. A — A failing backlight or inverter.** With refresh rate already ruled out as a cause, this chapter identifies backlight (more common) or inverter failure as the remaining hardware-level explanation for flickering.

**44. A — Display burn-in; the monitor needs replacement.** A persistent ghost image regardless of current content, on an aging OLED panel, matches this chapter's description of burn-in exactly, for which replacement is the only fix.

**45. B — Disconnect the battery from the motherboard, wait briefly, then reconnect it.** This is the first recommended fix in this chapter even when a device won't power on plugged into a confirmed-working outlet, since a battery fault can also block power-on while plugged in.

**46. B — Power it off immediately, disconnect it from any charger, and handle the battery with care.** This is the correct immediate response to a swollen battery per this chapter, regardless of whether the device still appears to function normally.

**47. B — The point stick not re-centering properly; move it back and forth or recalibrate.** Slow, self-directed drift (versus erratic jumps while typing) combined with the presence of a point stick matches this chapter's specific description of cursor drift, distinct from a touchpad ghost cursor.

**48. A — Whether the network card or connectivity hardware itself is failing.** With toggle, software enablement, and signal strength all ruled out, this chapter identifies failing network hardware as the remaining explanation for persistent intermittent drops.

**49. B — The wired and wireless adapters share a card and the active wireless connection is blocking the wired path; disable Wi-Fi.** This is a direct match to this chapter's explanation of wired/wireless conflicts on a single shared network card.

**50. B — Without a stated IP rating, it may or may not survive; it should be treated as unrated and handled with the standard water-damage procedure.** This chapter is explicit that devices without any IP rating carry no guarantee either way, and should go through the standard water-damage steps (power off, dry, air-dry or rice method).

**51. B — Check the OS and app versions for how current they are, and review recently installed apps, before deciding on a fix.** With no physical damage and storage well above the 1 GB danger threshold, an outdated OS/apps or a specific problem app are the more likely causes this chapter flags before jumping to reset or replacement.

**52. B — Quarantine the system to prevent it from spreading to other machines.** Quarantine is explicitly the second of the five malware-removal steps, coming immediately after identification and before any remediation work begins.

**53. B — A broken, loose, or shorting printhead cable — secure or replace the cable.** This chapter's impact-printer table specifically distinguishes an intermittent blank-line symptom (cable-related) from a constant one (a stuck printhead pin), based on exactly this intermittent/constant distinction.

**54. B — The rough paper is acting like sandpaper and wearing the rollers, or the separation pads need adjustment for the new stock.** This chapter explicitly identifies overly rough paper as behaving like sandpaper against pickup rollers, contributing to exactly this multi-sheet feed symptom.

**55.
