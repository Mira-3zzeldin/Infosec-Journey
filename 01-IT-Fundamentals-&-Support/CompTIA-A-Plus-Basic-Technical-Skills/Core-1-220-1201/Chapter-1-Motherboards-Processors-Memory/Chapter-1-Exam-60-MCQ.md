# ANSWER KEY WITH EXPLANATIONS

---
---

**1. A — Advanced Technology Extended**
ATX = Advanced Technology Extended. Developed by Intel in the mid-1990s. Know this acronym cold.

---

**2. B — 12" × 9.6"**
Standard ATX is 12" × 9.6" (305mm × 244mm). microATX is 9.6" × 9.6" square. Mini-ITX is 6.7" × 6.7".

---

**3. D — Mobile-ITX**
The ITX family from smallest to largest: Mobile-ITX (2.4"×2.4") → Pico-ITX (3.9"×2.8") → Nano-ITX (4.7"×4.7") → Mini-ITX (6.7"×6.7"). Mobile-ITX is for embedded systems and smartphones.

---

**4. B — Unified Extensible Firmware Interface**
UEFI = Unified Extensible Firmware Interface. It replaced the legacy BIOS starting in 2002.

---

**5. B — Provide backup power to retain BIOS/UEFI settings when the PC is off**
The CMOS battery powers the CMOS chip (which stores BIOS settings, date/time, etc.) when the system is unplugged. Without it, all settings are lost every time the PC is turned off.

---

**6. C — 288**
DDR4 DIMMs have 288 pins. DDR5 DIMMs also have 288 pins. DDR3 and DDR2 have 240 pins. DDR has 184 pins. Memorize the DDR DIMM pin table.

---

**7. C — ARM**
ARM (Advanced RISC Machine) is the architecture used in virtually all smartphones and tablets due to its small size, low heat output, and power efficiency. x64/x86 are CISC architectures used in desktops and laptops.

---

**8. B — Error-Correcting Code**
ECC = Error-Correcting Code. It detects single- and double-bit errors and corrects single-bit errors. Essential for servers.

---

**9. C — L3**
L3 cache is the largest (8–16 MB typical) and slowest of the three cache levels, and it is shared among all processor cores. L1 is the fastest and smallest; L2 is dedicated per core and between L1 and L3 in speed.

---

**10. D — 262**
DDR5 SODIMM = 262 pins. DDR4 SODIMM = 260. DDR3 SODIMM = 204. DDR / DDR2 SODIMM = 200. SDR SODIMM = 144.

---

**11. B — PCIe**
PCIe (PCI Express) was designed to replace both PCI and the video-only AGP standard. It has no plug compatibility with either.

---

**12. C — 600 MBps**
SATA III runs at 6 Gbps. Using 8b/10b encoding (divide by 10): 6 Gbps ÷ 10 = 600 MBps. SATA I = 150 MBps; SATA II = 300 MBps.

---

**13. C — LGA**
LGA (Land Grid Array) places spring-loaded pins on the motherboard, with flat contact pads ("lands") on the CPU. PGA (Pin Grid Array) is the opposite — pins on the CPU, holes in the socket.

---

**14. D — 1.1V**
DDR5 runs at 1.1V. Each DDR generation reduces voltage: DDR=2.5V, DDR2=1.8V, DDR3=1.5V, DDR4=1.2V, DDR5=1.1V.

---

**15. B — Running the CPU faster than the manufacturer's rated speed**
Overclocking means manually increasing CPU speed beyond its factory rating. It requires more voltage and generates more heat, potentially shortening CPU life and voiding warranty.

---

**16. C — SRAM**
SRAM (Static RAM) does not need a refresh signal, is faster (access times under 10ns vs. 40ns+ for DRAM), and is used for cache memory. It is more expensive and complex than DRAM.

---

**17. C — They share the same mounting holes and rear I/O position as ATX boards**
Mini-ITX boards have four mounting holes that align with ATX/microATX and place rear I/O in the same location, making them ATX case-compatible. They are, however, smaller with fewer slots.

---

**18. B — Power-On Self-Test**
POST = Power-On Self-Test. It is a series of hardware checks performed by the BIOS/UEFI every time the computer starts, before the OS loads.

---

**19. C — 240**
DDR3 DIMMs have 240 pins. DDR2 also has 240 pins. DDR has 184. DDR4 and DDR5 have 288. The notch position differentiates them.

---

**20. C — Installing device drivers for the OS**
Installing device drivers is a function of the OS and OS utilities, not the BIOS/UEFI. BIOS/UEFI handles POST, boot sequence, hardware initialization, and security — but not OS-level driver management.

---

**21. C — 64 GBps**
PCIe 4.0 x16: each direction is ~2 GBps per lane × 16 lanes = ~32 GBps per direction. Bidirectional total = ~64 GBps. Each PCIe generation doubles the previous: 1.0 x16 = 8 GBps bidirectional, 2.0 = 16, 3.0 = ~32, 4.0 = ~64.

---

**22. Explanation: C. Install two modules in the two blue slots (matching color) while leaving the black slots empty**  
On motherboards with an alternating color layout, slots with matching colors belong to different memory channels (Channel A and Channel B) but form the same logical bank. To activate dual-channel performance, standard practice dictates populating the primary matching-color slots (the blue pair) first, which bridges both channels in parallel to double your bandwidth. Conversely, filling adjacent, different-colored slots (like slot 1 and slot 2) locks the RAM into the same single channel, cutting potential throughput in half. Always consult your specific motherboard manual to confirm which color pair represents the primary banks.

---

**23. C — It can damage the ECC functionality of the motherboard**
Adding non-ECC RAM to an ECC-enabled motherboard can damage ECC functionality. The reverse (ECC RAM in non-ECC board) works but without error correction. Always match ECC to ECC in servers.

---

**24. B — x86 is 32-bit; x64 is 64-bit and newer**
Despite the misleading numbers, x86 = 32-bit (named after Intel 80386) and x64 = 64-bit (newer). Both use CISC (not ARM/RISC). Both Intel and AMD make x64 processors.

---

**25. B — PCIe uses point-to-point lanes, giving each device its own dedicated bandwidth**
Unlike PCI's shared bus (where all devices compete for the same bandwidth), PCIe provides dedicated point-to-point connections via a switching fabric. Each device gets its full rated bandwidth.

---

**26. B — The system effectively operates with a 128-bit memory bus**
Dual-channel doubles the effective memory bus width from 64-bit to 128-bit by coordinating two modules simultaneously. Identical matching modules in the correct slots enable this; different modules would work in single-channel mode.

---

**27. C — The card will function at PCIe 3.0 speeds**
PCIe is backward and forward compatible. A higher-generation card in a lower-generation slot negotiates to the slower speed. No damage occurs; functionality is retained at reduced performance.

---

**28. C — LGA 1700**
The Intel Core i5-13600K is a 13th generation (Raptor Lake) processor, which uses the LGA 1700 socket. LGA 1200 is for 10th and 11th gen (Comet Lake, Rocket Lake). Socket AM4 is AMD.

---

**29. C — Verifies digital signatures of boot files before executing them**
Secure Boot checks digital signatures of option ROMs, the boot loader, and OS boot files before executing them. It prevents tampered or unauthorized code from running at startup.

---

**30. B — CISC processors can perform more operations per instruction but may take more clock cycles per instruction**
CISC (x86/x64) instructions are complex and can perform multiple operations per instruction, but each instruction may take several clock cycles. RISC (ARM) uses simpler instructions that generally complete in one clock cycle but need more instructions to complete a task.

---

**31. B — To fill microscopic air gaps between the CPU and heat sink, improving heat transfer**
Thermal paste bridges microscopic gaps between the CPU surface and the heat sink base, eliminating pockets of air that would otherwise act as insulators, maximizing thermal conductivity.

---

**32. B — Rated bit rate ÷ 10 = MBps throughput**
SATA uses 8b/10b encoding. For every 8 data bits, 2 overhead bits are added, requiring 10 bits to be transmitted. So: 1.5 Gbps ÷ 10 = 150 MBps; 3 Gbps ÷ 10 = 300 MBps; 6 Gbps ÷ 10 = 600 MBps.

---

**33. B — A TPM is a dedicated chip on the motherboard; an HSM is a separate device that can serve broader functions including network-wide crypto services**
TPM is integrated directly onto the motherboard as a security coprocessor. HSM is an external device (USB, PCIe, or server appliance) that can replicate TPM functions and also provide crypto services at network scale. They can be used together in layers.

---

**34. C — The CPU, BIOS/UEFI, and operating system must all support it**
Hardware virtualization requires a three-way combination: CPU support (Intel VT or AMD-V), BIOS/UEFI enabling of the feature, and OS support (e.g., Windows with Hyper-V). Missing any one element prevents virtualization from working.

---

**35. C — 260**
DDR4 SODIMM = 260 pins. DDR5 SODIMM = 262. DDR3 SODIMM = 204. DDR/DDR2 SODIMM = 200.

---

**36. B — The fan will work, but speed monitoring and control (PWM) are lost**
A 3-pin connector in a 4-pin header fits correctly (same pin positions for power and ground). The fan runs, but Pin 4 (PWM speed control signal) is unused, so the motherboard cannot regulate the fan speed dynamically.

---

**37. B — M.2 is a form factor that can support SATA, USB, or PCIe buses**
M.2 is purely a physical form factor/connector standard. It is bus-agnostic and can carry SATA, USB, or PCIe signals depending on the device and slot support. Speed depends on the bus used, not M.2 itself.

---

**38. B — To prevent unauthorized users from introducing malware via flash drives**
Disabling USB ports via BIOS/UEFI is a security measure that stops malicious USB devices (like infected flash drives) from being used to introduce malware, even if someone gains physical access to the machine.

---

**39. C — Remove all old thermal paste, clean the surfaces, and apply fresh paste before reinstalling**
Old, hardened thermal paste loses its heat-conducting efficiency. Always remove it completely, clean both the CPU and heat sink surfaces, and apply fresh thermal paste. Never reuse old compound or layer new paste on top of old.

---

**40. B — Makes each physical core appear as two logical processors**
Intel's HTT (Hyper-Threading Technology) uses superscalar CPU architecture to allow one physical core to handle two threads simultaneously. The OS sees twice the number of logical processors as physical cores. Visible in Windows Task Manager.

---

**41. C — The CPU heat sink has inadequate or dried-out thermal paste; remove the heat sink, clean surfaces, and apply new thermal paste**
Temperature-related shutdowns with working fans most commonly point to thermal paste failure between the CPU and heat sink. After years of use, paste dries and loses conductivity. RAM or PSU issues would have different symptoms. OS corruption wouldn't cause thermal shutdowns.

---

**42. B — The SSD will function, but at PCIe 3.0 speeds**
PCIe is generation-backward compatible. A PCIe 4.0 device in a PCIe 3.0 slot will negotiate down to PCIe 3.0 speeds. There is no damage and no failure — only reduced performance.

---

**43. C — Do not install the non-ECC modules; doing so could damage the ECC functionality of the motherboard**
The chapter explicitly states: "Adding non-ECC RAM to an ECC motherboard can damage the ECC functionality of the motherboard." This is the opposite of installing ECC RAM in a non-ECC board (which is safe, just without correction). In a production server, this risk is unacceptable — source ECC RAM.

---

**44. B — The AMD CPU is physically incompatible with the Intel LGA 1700 socket**
Intel and AMD sockets are entirely incompatible. LGA 1700 is an Intel socket. The AMD Ryzen 9 7900X uses AMD's AM5 socket. The CPU physically will not fit, and the electrical specs are different.

---

**45. B — The two modules are in the same channel; they should be in A1 and B1 to use dual-channel**
On most dual-channel motherboards, A-slots are one channel and B-slots are another. Filling A1 and A2 keeps both modules in the same channel (single-channel operation). Dual-channel requires one module in each channel — typically A1 + B1 or A2 + B2 (or as specified in motherboard documentation).

---

**46. C — The BIOS/UEFI firmware is corrupt or incomplete; the flash failed mid-process**
A power outage during a BIOS flash can corrupt the firmware, leaving the system unable to boot (no POST, no display, no beeps from a functional speaker). This is why using a UPS is critical when flashing BIOS. Some motherboards have failsafe partitions or recovery jumpers, but the root cause is corrupt firmware.

---

**47. C — 32-bit operating systems can only address approximately 4 GB of RAM**
32-bit (x86) operating systems have a maximum addressable RAM of 2³² bytes = 4 GB (with some of that reserved for hardware). No matter how much RAM is physically installed, a 32-bit OS won't use more than ~4 GB. Upgrading to a 64-bit OS resolves this.

---

**48. B — More physical cores and hardware virtualization support (Intel VT or AMD-V)**
Running multiple VMs simultaneously requires: (1) enough CPU cores so each VM gets dedicated resources (more cores = more VMs running well), and (2) hardware virtualization support (Intel VT or AMD-V) which offloads virtualization overhead to the CPU instead of software, dramatically improving VM performance. ARM and Secure Boot are irrelevant here.

---

**49. B — The front-panel USB header cable is not connected or is connected to the wrong header on the new motherboard**
Rear USB ports connect directly to the motherboard's I/O panel (built-in). Front USB ports connect via a header cable from the case to the motherboard. After a board replacement, this cable must be reconnected to the correct USB header on the new board. BIOS USB settings affect all USB; a missing header connection would only affect front ports.

---

**50. C — 4**
Quad-channel memory requires four modules operating in sync simultaneously. One bank = one module in today's 64-bit systems. Four banks/modules are needed for quad-channel, giving an effective 256-bit bus width.

---

**51. C — Remove the CPU heat sink, clean off the old thermal paste, and apply fresh compound**
The system ran fine for two years, then began thermal shutdowns. Case fans work. This is a classic sign of dried-out thermal paste that has lost conductivity over time. The CPU heats up too fast under even moderate load. Fresh thermal paste is the most direct and likely fix before replacing other expensive components.

---

**52. B — Set the internal drive as the only boot device and set a BIOS/Admin password to lock BIOS settings**
The correct answer is two complementary settings: (1) Remove all external media from the boot order so only the internal drive can boot, AND (2) set a BIOS admin/supervisor password so an attacker cannot walk up to the machine, enter BIOS, and re-enable USB boot. Secure Boot validates what boots but doesn't prevent changing boot order without a BIOS password. TPM handles encryption, not boot order access.

---

**53. B — Remove the CMOS battery for a few minutes to drain CMOS memory, then reinstall it**
Removing the CMOS battery drains the CMOS chip of power, causing it to lose all stored settings — including the BIOS password. After reinstalling the battery and powering on, BIOS returns to factory defaults. A CMOS reset jumper on the motherboard serves the same purpose. You cannot reset BIOS passwords from within a running OS.

---

**54. B — No; a motherboard supports only one DDR generation, and the physical key notch prevents DDR4 from fitting a DDR5 slot**
Motherboards are physically designed for one DDR type. Each DDR generation has a unique key notch position, preventing a wrong module from being seated. DDR4 and DDR5 use the same 288-pin count on DIMMs but have different notch positions — DDR4 modules will not fit into DDR5 slots.

---

**55. B — A multisocket motherboard with the appropriate number of CPU sockets**
Multisocket motherboards have two or more CPU sockets, allowing multiple physical CPUs. This is used in servers and high-end workstations for maximum compute power. Standard ATX, microATX, and Mini-ITX consumer boards have only one CPU socket.

---

**56. C — Replace the entire motherboard**
Laptop CPUs are nearly always soldered directly onto the motherboard, meaning there is no removable socket. If the CPU fails, the motherboard must be replaced. This is a core fact about mobile device maintenance.

---

**57. B — A POST failure indicating a hardware problem; consult the motherboard manufacturer's documentation to decode the beep pattern**
Beep codes at startup are generated by POST when it detects a hardware failure. They occur before the OS loads, so this is a hardware (not software) problem. Each BIOS/motherboard manufacturer has its own beep code definitions. Common causes include failed RAM, GPU, or CPU. Decoding the beeps is the first diagnostic step.

---

**58. B — ECC RAM, because it can detect and correct single-bit errors, preventing data corruption**
In financial or other mission-critical applications, a single-bit memory error could cause silent data corruption — incorrect calculations that are never flagged. ECC RAM detects and corrects these errors automatically. Non-ECC RAM with parity only detects errors (no correction). SRAM is used for cache, not system RAM.

---

**59. C — Inspect and likely replace the thermal paste between the CPU and heat sink**
After cleaning dust (which you've done), with the CPU still running at 95°C under light load, the thermal interface between CPU and heat sink is the next most likely failure point. Thermal paste degrades over years. Replacing it is the logical next step before investing in new hardware.

---

**60. B — microATX, because it fits in ATX cases and offers more slots than Mini-ITX while being smaller than ATX**
microATX (9.6"×9.6") fits in standard ATX cases (compatible mounting holes), supports PCIe x16 for a GPU, and provides a middle ground between the limited expansion of Mini-ITX and the full size of ATX — ideal for a compact gaming build that still needs GPU support. Mobile-ITX does not fit ATX cases.
